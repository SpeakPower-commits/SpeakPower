Method

The formulas, not a black box.

A score you cannot interrogate is a horoscope. Here is exactly how this one is computed.

Readability

Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words / sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables / words) Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level = 0.39 × (words / sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables / words) − 15.59

Syllables are counted with the standard vowel-group heuristic, which is accurate on ordinary prose and less so on proper nouns — which is why the word and sentence counts are reported alongside the score rather than hidden. Below 30 words the formulas are not reliable, so the tool refuses to run rather than returning a confident number it cannot support.

Each component scores 0 to 1 — full marks inside its band, tapering linearly outside it — then the weighted sum is scaled to 100. Abstract-noun density is measured and reported but deliberately left out of the composite: it overlaps heavily with readability, and counting it twice would punish the same sentence twice.

Flesch, R. (1948). A new readability yardstick. Journal of Applied Psychology, 32(3), 221–233.

Kincaid, J.P., Fishburne, R.P., Rogers, R.L., & Chissom, B.S. (1975). Derivation of new readability formulas for Navy enlisted personnel. Research Branch Report 8-75.

The hedge and jargon lexicons are hand-built and deliberately short. A long buzzword list produces false positives on words that are load-bearing in a real sentence.

The limit of it

This measures mechanics. It cannot tell you if the thesis is right.

Every number here is about how the text is written. None of them can tell you whether you are making the right claim to the right audience, whether your evidence would survive a hostile question, or whether the story is worth telling at all. Those are the questions worth paying for — and they need a conversation, not a formula.

Score well here and you have clean mechanics. That is the floor, not the ceiling.
